The Tuskegee Airmen

They flew two wars — one against the Nazis over Germany, one against racism at home. The runway they built is still open. Mission Aviation Academy exists because of it.

Origins

Thirty Years of a Closed Sky

The Wright Brothers invented the airplane in 1903. By the end of 1909, the Army had its first airplanes and its first pilots. For more than thirty years afterwards, however, there were no African American pilots in the American military. Racism was the primary reason. The War Department did not allow African Americans to serve as military pilots for decades.

This is the story of the first Black military pilots in American history: the Tuskegee Airmen. They were not only the pilots, but also the ground personnel who served in their units or at their bases — and they outnumbered the pilots more than ten to one.

1941
The War Department activated the 99th Pursuit Squadron (later 99th Fighter Squadron) at Chanute Field, Illinois — but it had no pilots until a year later, when it moved to Tuskegee.
1941–42
Primary flight training at Moton Field, a Tuskegee Institute facility on grass runways, with African American flight instructors — many of whom had taught civilian pilot training at Tuskegee before 1941. Advanced training followed at Tuskegee Army Air Field, commanded by Colonel Noel F. Parrish.
~1,000
African American pilots completed flight training — out of far more who entered. Less than half of cadets who entered flying training graduated and received their wings, becoming officers in the Army Air Forces.
Combat Record

Red Tails Over Europe

Most of the Tuskegee Airmen pilots served in two units: the 332nd Fighter Group and the 477th Bombardment Group.

The 99th Fighter Squadron was the first to deploy to combat in 1943, attached to various white fighter groups in North Africa, Sicily, and Italy, flying P-40s. In early 1944 the 332nd Fighter Group deployed to Italy with its 100th, 301st, and 302nd Fighter Squadrons. In the summer of 1944, the 99th was reassigned to the 332nd — making it the only fighter group in World War II with four squadrons, with more pilots and planes than any other.

Reassigned to the Fifteenth Air Force (which flew heavy four-engine bombers such as B-17 Flying Fortresses and B-24 Liberators), the Tuskegee Airmen flew P-47s and then P-51 Mustangs with red tails — to distinguish them from the six other fighter escort groups. They are remembered most for their missions escorting bombers into central Europe, over Germany, Austria, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, Romania, and other Nazi-occupied countries.

112
Enemy aircraft shot down by war's end
95
Tuskegee Airmen earned Distinguished Flying Crosses
27
Bombers lost to enemy (vs. avg. 46 for other groups)
179
Bomber escort missions — lost bombers on only 7

Sometimes the Tuskegee Airmen fighters were outnumbered not only by the number of bombers they had to protect, but also by the number of enemy fighters sent to shoot them down. Yet they lost significantly fewer bombers than any other escort group in the Fifteenth Air Force.

"They proved beyond a shadow of a doubt that they could fly the most advanced combat fighters as skillfully and effectively as the white pilots."

— Daniel Haulman, PhD · Air Force Historical Research Agency

The most memorable mission the Tuskegee Airmen flew was to Berlin on March 24, 1945 — the longest mission ever flown by the Fifteenth Air Force. Three Tuskegee Airmen — 1st Lt. Roscoe C. Brown, 2nd Lt. Charles V. Brantley, and 1st Lt. Earl R. Lane — each shot down a German Me-262 jet that day. It was the first time Tuskegee Airmen shot down German jets.

477th Bombardment Group · Freeman Field

The Double Victory

The bomber pilots who trained at Tuskegee served in the 477th Bombardment Group, with its four squadrons: the 616th, 617th, 618th, and 619th. Because the group was activated late in the war — and it took longer to train bomber crews than fighter pilots — the 477th never deployed overseas. It flew twin-engine B-25s, the same kind the Doolittle Raiders flew in the first attack on Japan.

The 477th should be remembered for a famous episode: the Freeman Field Mutiny. More than 100 Black officers at Freeman Field, Indiana refused to accept a segregated officers' club policy and were arrested and reprimanded. All arrested African American officers were eventually exonerated, and the white commander was replaced by the most famous Tuskegee Airmen of all: Colonel Benjamin O. Davis, Jr. — who had earlier commanded the 99th Fighter Squadron and the 332nd Fighter Group.

Gen. Daniel "Chappie" James Jr. Chapter

General Daniel "Chappie" James, Jr.

The first four-star Black general in the Air Force — or in any of the armed forces of the United States — was General Daniel "Chappie" James, Jr., who had been a member of the 477th Bombardment Group. The Tuskegee Airmen Inc. chapter that works alongside Mission Aviation Academy bears his name.

His mentor and predecessor, Colonel Benjamin O. Davis, Jr., attended West Point, and his father — Benjamin O. Davis, Sr. — was the first Black general in the U.S. Army. Benjamin O. Davis, Jr. eventually became the first Black general in the Air Force. A lineage of firsts, earned against every institutional headwind imaginable.

Legacy

Executive Order 9981 and the Congressional Gold Medal

Although much of the Tuskegee Airmen story is about the pilots, most of the Tuskegee Airmen were not pilots. They served on the ground — as crew chiefs, maintenance personnel, armorers, intelligence officers, clerks, transportation personnel, instructors, nurses, parachute riggers, and weathermen. There were almost 1,000 Tuskegee Airmen pilots, but more than 13,000 other Tuskegee Airmen who were not pilots — yet on whom the pilots depended to accomplish their missions.

By demonstrating they were the equal of the whites in the Army Air Forces — and could fly the same kinds of airplanes on the same kinds of missions, just as well as anyone else — the Tuskegee Airmen helped convince the leaders of the Army Air Forces and the Air Force, which emerged in 1947, that there was no good reason for segregation in the service.

When President Harry S. Truman issued Executive Order 9981 in 1948, mandating the desegregation of the Armed Forces, the Air Force was already in the process of integrating — already training Black pilots alongside white pilots at Williams Air Force Base in Arizona.

In March 2007, President George W. Bush presided at a ceremony in the Capitol at which a Congressional Gold Medal was presented collectively to the Tuskegee Airmen, recognizing their achievements. They fought two wars: one against the Nazis overseas and one against racism at home. It was indeed a double victory.

The Living Chain

From the 332nd to the Students We Serve

The three living Tuskegee anchors woven through Your Aviation Blueprint — Lt. Col. George Hardy, Flight Officer Daniel Keel, and Rev. Reginald A. Davis — are direct continuations of the history above. Their relationship with LCDR Ricardo "Tattoo" Foster was not symbolic. It was personal mentorship that redirected the trajectory of his life and the lives of the students he now serves.

Foster grew up in Jamaica, West Indies, and later moved to Brooklyn, New York, with little more than faith and determination. Aviation was nowhere in his world — not in his neighborhood, his school, or his imagination — until his mother, an educator, opened a door during a trip to the United States. One airplane ride gave him his first glimpse of what was possible. That glimpse became a beginning.

He entered aviation through August Martin High School in Queens — an aviation-focused school named for the first Black commercial airline pilot in U.S. history. He enlisted in the United States Navy in 1990 as an E-1 Aviation Boatswain's Mate Apprentice, earned his way to Lieutenant Commander over 28 years, and returned to Tampa to build a school that opens that same sky to everyone.

Mission Aviation Academy is that school. Every Saturday at Tampa North Aero Park (X39), the runway they built is in use.
